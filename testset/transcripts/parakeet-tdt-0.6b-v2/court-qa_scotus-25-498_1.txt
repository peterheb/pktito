ERISA's duty of prudence is inherently flexible.
As a result, this Court has consistently declined to inject judicially crafted bright line rules into a determination of whether a complaint's allegations plausibly allege imprudence.
Categorical rules, as this court put it colorfully in Dudenhofer, do not readily divide the plausible sheep from the meritless goats because they are inconsistent with the careful, context-sensitive inquiry that both ERISA and Rule 8 require.
The Ninth Circuit's meaningful benchmark requirement is just such a rule.
Under it, a court first asks whether a complaint's performance allegations on their own.
Can plausibly demonstrate imprudence by identifying a virtually indistinguishable fund that performed better.
If the complaint fails to identify that specific fund, the performance allegations are discarded.
And that is true even if the complaint relies on benchmarks selected by the fiduciaries themselves or comparators identified by benchmarking experts.
In the respondent's view, this rule is consistent with the ordinary pleading framework set forth in Twambly and Iqbal, but under that framework,
Courts must assess all non-conclusory allegations together in determining if a complaint plausibly states a claim.
The meaningful benchmark requirement precludes that assessment.

Everyone agrees that without more, a complaint merely alleging that one fund performed better than another won't survive a motion to dismiss.
